Quake spends most of its frame in two short loops: one that draws a horizontal span of a textured surface, and one that writes the span's depth. The span engine is those two loops in hardware. It takes a surface's description and a list of spans from the CPU, and writes perspective-correct, lit, RGB565 pixels into SDRAM at one pixel per clock, while the CPU gets on with the rest of the frame. It doubled Quake's frame rate, and turned out to be useful for three other games.
{: .lede}

## What it draws

Quake's software renderer doesn't draw polygons. It sorts the visible surfaces into spans, horizontal runs of pixels that each belong to one surface, and hands those to `D_DrawSpans8`. For each surface, three quantities vary linearly across the screen: s/z and t/z (the texture coordinates divided by depth) and 1/z. Each is a plane, an origin plus a step per pixel in x and in y. A span's pixels need s and t themselves, which means a division per pixel. Quake divides every 8 pixels and steps linearly in between, which is close enough that nobody notices.

The engine does exactly that, every 16 pixels instead of 8:

{% include m20/figure.html src="span-persp.svg" wide=true
   alt="The texture coordinate along a span: exact at every 16th pixel, linear in between, against the exact curve"
   caption="Along one span, the texture coordinate is exact at each dot and steps linearly between them. The error is a fraction of a texel." %}

The division is a pipeline of its own: leading zeros, a 1,025-entry reciprocal table with linear interpolation, a 32 × 18 multiply, a 64-bit shift, a clamp. Eight clocks deep, and a new operand every clock. While one 16-pixel segment's pixels stream out, the sequencer is already working out the next segment's ends, so a long span runs at a pixel per clock with no gaps.

## The pipeline

{% include m20/figure.html src="span-pipeline.svg" wide=true
   alt="The span engine's pipeline: CPU, FIFO, span core, texel cache, palette and light, write buffers, SDRAM"
   caption="Everything runs on the CPU's 60 MHz clock; only the texel cache's fill port and the write-back register are on the SDRAM controller's 120 MHz side." %}

- **Commands** go through a 512-entry FIFO, as `{register, value}` pairs written to an I/O window. A surface is a few register writes (the three planes with their exponents, the texture, its extents), then one 32-bit word per span: `{count, v, u}`, up to 1024 pixels long, anywhere on a 1024 × 512 screen. A register write waits until the pixels in flight are done, so a new surface can't overtake the old one's spans. The CPU only stalls if the FIFO is full.
- **The span core** turns each span into a stream of texel addresses and 16-bit depths.
- **The texel cache** started at 2 KB, 32 lines of 64 bytes, direct-mapped; it's 8 KB now, 128 lines, with the index hashed by the tag's low bits so that a wide texture's rows don't all land on the same few lines. A miss fetches one line from SDRAM through the engine's own port on the controller.
- **The palette** is 256 RGB888 entries in block RAM (two banks of them now: Tomb Raider's underwater faces use the second). A lit texel's colour is multiplied by its brightness (three DSP multipliers) and packed into RGB565.
- **Write buffers**, two 64-byte lines each for the frame buffer and the z buffer, collect pixels and write them back as masked line writes when a span leaves the line. With the depth test on (below), the z buffer goes through a small z cache instead, which fetches the next span's lines while the current one draws. When the engine reports idle, every pixel is in SDRAM.

The engine runs on the CPU's clock. At the SDRAM's 120 MHz its control paths wouldn't close timing, and at 60 MHz it has all the speed it needs: a pixel per clock is already more than the SDRAM can take as reads and writes from everything else.

## Lighting: no surface cache

Quake's renderer doesn't light pixels as it draws them. It builds a *surface cache*: for each visible surface, a copy of its texture with the lightmap baked in, at the right mip level, and the span drawer reads from that. Building those surfaces on the CPU took a good share of the frame.

So the engine learned to light pixels itself. In lit mode, the span reads the texture directly (power-of-two sizes, wrapping by mask) and takes the light from the surface's lightmap, which the engine loads into block RAM itself (up to 31 × 31 samples, 2<sup>k</sup> texels apart). The light is bilinear at each segment's ends and stepped linearly in between, like the texture coordinates. Then the palette's colour is multiplied by the brightness, where 128 means as-is and 256 is twice as bright. Colours 224 to 255 stay unlit: those are Quake's fullbright colours, the lamps and lava.

That's GL-style lighting rather than Quake's colormap, multiplying RGB instead of picking a darker palette entry. It looks slightly different from the original renderer, but it removes the surface cache's build entirely, and it made the frame RGB565 for free. The video engine doubles 16-bit lines horizontally the same way it does 8-bit ones.

## Verification: one C model, bit for bit

Before any RTL, the engine was a C model inside the Quake port: the same arithmetic in integers, the same segment lengths, the same rounding. Quake could draw with Quake's own drawers, with the model, or (later) with the engine, switched at run time. Getting the model right on screen first meant the RTL had a reference that was known to draw Quake correctly.

That C file became the hardware's specification. A test generator built on it produces random surfaces and spans (including the awkward ones: steep surfaces, spans past the old screen limits, lightmaps at the edges) and the RTL testbench compares every pixel: hundreds of thousands per seed, zero differences, with random backpressure on the output. A second test runs the whole SoC with the CPU drawing the same spans through the model and comparing them with the engine's output in SDRAM.

Later a second program grew on top of the model: an emulator of the whole engine at the register level, with the FIFO's commands, the lightmap loads, the edge walk, the depth test and its z cache. Each game's PC build can draw through it as if through the hardware, and be checked before it goes near a board; on recorded Tomb Raider frames and random command traces it matches the RTL bit for bit, depths included. What it can't show is timing: it finishes every command at once, so it's never busy (see below for why that matters).

The model caught two bugs in itself on the way. Its segment ends needed clamping so that stepping could never take s or t below zero (a read one byte before the texture), and the last segment's step needed rounding toward zero. Both would have been miserable to find in hardware.

## Making it fit

The first RTL version of the span core used 1,680 ALUs, which was too much even on the 20K. The fixes were the usual ones: one shared adder for all the multiply-adds, sequenced over the clocks the division takes anyway; a 32 × 11 multiplier instead of 32 × 32, since the other operand is a pixel position; cheaper rounding. It came down to 160 ALUs. The surface registers moved from flip-flops into a small LUT RAM with two read ports, which saved about 400 registers and 2 MHz of CPU clock.

Two of the block RAM rules from [part 2](../cpu/) applied here too: the palette and lightmap reads mustn't sit on the address being written, and every pipeline stage's value must hold in a stall (without the enables, 134 of 1,928 pixels came out with the wrong colour in simulation). And the request to the SDRAM controller needed its flop: before it, on some layouts, an engine write went to the wrong cache line, and Quake's data got silently overwritten.

The first full engine was about 4,300 logic cells and 10 block RAMs: the second biggest block on the chip, a bit more than half the CPU. It has grown since (below), and to make room at 60 MHz it was trimmed again: a narrower clamp in the division, the lightmap blend in a DSP block, and one multiply-add for every texel offset, lit or not.

## What the other games added

The engine grew as the other games needed things:

- **Edges.** An EDGE command gives the engine a face's left and right edges (x and a step per row, 16.16) and a range of rows, and it makes the spans itself, clamped to a clip window; an edge that isn't sent again carries on from where it got to. Tomb Raider sends half the FIFO words that way, and the CPU does no per-row work at all.
- **A depth test**, with the z cache, and a skip for texel 0 (the see-through pixels), for Tomb Raider's faces and Quake's models.
- **Lit textures of any width.** Lit textures used to be powers of two, wrapping by mask, and Quake's model skins aren't. A width register now puts the rows any distance apart, through the same multiply-add as the unlit offsets, so the skins are read where Quake loaded them. Padding them to powers of two instead cost 0.7 MB, which pushed models out of Quake's cache.
- **One address stream for depth.** Part of the room for 60 MHz came from the depth buffer: inside the engine a depth goes by its pixel's frame address, and the depth buffer's offset from the frame is only added as a line leaves for SDRAM. So the depth buffer has to sit a multiple of 2 KB away from the frame, with the same stride, and a depth stride of zero means no depth writes at all.

## Using it from software

The engine writes SDRAM behind the CPU's back, so a program has to keep the data cache honest:

- **Before:** textures, lightmaps and anything else the engine reads must be written back from the cache (`cbo.clean`).
- **After:** any part of the frame the CPU might read or write must not be sitting in its cache with stale contents (`cbo.flush` before the engine writes there, or a wait for the engine before the CPU touches it).
- **Waiting** is a status register: the engine is busy until everything it was given is drawn and written back.
- **The buffer addresses only while it's idle.** The frame and depth base registers take effect at once. Quake once moved the frame base to the other display buffer while the engine was still writing back the last frame's model depths, which then went a display buffer away from the depth buffer, into Quake's own variables: traps in the client and sound code within minutes of play, and nothing in the emulator, which is never busy.

Counters for pixels drawn, texel cache misses and busy cycles (in profiling builds: they were trimmed out of the normal one) make it easy to see whether the engine or the CPU is the limit, and in practice it's usually the CPU.

## What its cache likes

The engine is as fast as its texels arrive, and the texel cache was small and simple: 2 KB, direct-mapped, 64-byte lines. It was designed for Quake's surface cache, which a span reads more or less along its rows. Other games read textures differently (measured with the 2 KB cache):

<div class="table-wrap" markdown="1">

| Game | Textures | Texel misses | Why |
|---|---|--:|---|
| Descent | 64 × 64, a row per cache line | under 1% | a whole row in each line; half the texture fits |
| OpenLara | 256 × 256 pages, rows 256 bytes apart | about 10% | 8 rows of a face's texture fit before they evict each other |
| System Shock | 128 × 128 on floors | up to 51% | when a texture's rows run across the screen, every scanline crosses more rows than the cache holds |

</div>

Since then the cache grew to 8 KB with a hashed index; textures read across their rows still miss 10 to 40%. It also used to write depth always, which only Quake needed then; now a depth stride of zero switches the writes off. A two-way cache is the obvious next change. In none of the games is the engine the bottleneck yet, though: it's busy 25 to 40% of the time, and the rest of each frame is the CPU's.

## A Quake accelerator, or a general one?

m9b's rule was no game-specific hardware, and this started as exactly that: Quake's inner loop. What made it general is that the interface is a primitive, not a game. "Draw these spans of this textured, lit plane" is what every textured 3D renderer of the era does underneath, whatever it calls it. Descent's texture mapper, System Shock's wall and floor mappers and Tomb Raider's face rasterizer all reduce to it, and each port drives the same engine with a few hundred lines of setup code. [Part 4](../games/) is about those ports, and what it took to make games written for 8-bit screens share a 16-bit frame with it.
